Learn Yoga Flow Design with Phoebe · Session 3 of 6

Transitions that work

Every room has a few moves where it stalls. Half the students are in the next pose, the other half are still looking for their foot. Tonight we take the three moves that stall rooms most often (Downward dog to a lunge, standing to the floor and back, and turning from the front of the mat to the side), draw an options ladder for each, and learn to split an expensive move into two cheaper ones. Then we rewrite three of Rosa's worst moves.

Part 0

Where we start

Rosa is a constructed case: an invented, newly qualified teacher with a 60-minute Thursday flow class at the Harbour Room, an invented community studio. Three weeks in, she has noticed something. The room does not stall in the hard poses. It stalls at the same three moves every week: stepping forward from Downward dog, getting up off the floor after Bridge, and the turn from Triangle back into a Vinyasa. Each time, the class spreads out over several breaths, and her next cue lands on people who are not there yet.

Not medical advice. The guides on this page show common teaching options for moving between poses. They do not diagnose, treat or clear anyone for exercise. If a student has pain, dizziness, a recent injury or a health condition, they stop and talk to a clinician; you adapt the move and refer, you never assess. Intake and caution flags live in Yoga Course Design session 2 ↗, and session 6 here covers the warning signs.

Part 1 · covers Downward dog to a lunge

One move, four ways in

5 min live

On the Flow Bench, Downward dog to Crescent lunge costs 2 units (base and level) and Downward dog to Low lunge also costs 2 (base and level, the other way). On paper they are equal and cheap. In a real room, stepping the foot from Downward dog up between the hands is one of the most common places for a class to stall. An options ladder gives every student a route that works for their body tonight.

LiveTeach a transition like a pose3 min▶

New teachers spend their words on poses and rush the moves: "step your right foot forward and rise up into Crescent." That is two changes of state in one breath, given as one cue, to a room that is mid-air. Treat the move as a thing in its own right, and three habits follow.

Name it. "Now the step forward." Students who know a move is coming can prepare their hands and look down. Give it its own breath. One exhale to arrive, one breath to settle, before the next cue. The breath counts on these guides are planning values, and pairing an exhale with a step is a practitioner convention rather than a tested rule (Yoga Theory session 7 ↗ covers breath and movement in the arc of a class). Offer the slower route first. Cue option D, then "if you like, try stepping straight through." Students hear the supported route as the default and the harder one as the extra, never the other way round.

The wording itself, how to phrase and sequence a cue, belongs to Yoga Theory session 8 ↗. Here we decide which moves need the words.

Self-studyWhy the cheap move on paper can be the hardest2 min▶

The unit count measures how many states change: base, level, facing, side. It knows nothing about range of movement, balance or strength. Stepping straight through from Downward dog changes only two states, yet it asks the hip to bend deeply while the hands carry weight and one foot travels through the air. For a student with tight hips, a stiff back or long legs and short arms, the foot lands halfway and they have to shuffle it forward. That shuffle is the stall you see.

Option D costs exactly the same 2 units on the bench (Downward dog to Tabletop 2, Tabletop to Low lunge 0) but spends them over two moves with a rest in between, so it takes at least one more breath. That breath is a good trade in a mixed room. Why hips differ in range is functional anatomy, covered in Yoga Theory session 5 ↗; for planning, it is enough to know that the count and the difficulty are different things.

Real world

The shuffle after "step your foot between your hands". A familiar sight in any mixed class: a row of students walking a foot forward in four or five small steps, some grabbing an ankle, a few dropping a knee on their own. They have built their own ladder because the cue did not offer one. A teacher who names option D first turns that improvised scramble into a choice.

Part 2 · covers standing to the floor and back, Swain 2016

Down to the floor, and back up

5 min live

Session 1 counted floor trips: moves that cross between standing or hands-and-feet and kneeling, sitting or lying. Rosa's first draft has 10. This is the guide for doing one well. Going down is three stops, never a drop; coming up is the same route in reverse, with something to push on.

LiveFloor trips are where a room spreads in time3 min▶

In a standing line, everyone moves at roughly the same speed. On a floor trip the spread opens up: some students are seated in two breaths, others need five and a hand on a block. If you cue the next pose as soon as the fastest are down, the slowest are always one move behind, hurrying. Wait for the room, not for the front row.

Session 1 gave the base rates. Swain and McGwin counted yoga-related emergency department visits in the US, and in 2014 the rate for people aged 65 and over was 57.9 emergency visits per 100,000 participants, against 11.9 for ages 18 to 44. Older students are the ones most at risk, and getting down to the floor and back up asks the most of them. That is a reason to plan fewer floor trips and to give each one stops and support.

One thing the evidence does not say: no study we could find measured whether injuries happen more during transitions than in held poses. So the honest sentence is "floor trips ask the most of the people most at risk", never "most injuries happen in transitions".

Self-studyThree routes up, and how they count2 min▶

Via all fours and Downward dog. Easy seat to Tabletop 1, Tabletop to Downward dog 2, Downward dog to Standing forward fold 2, fold to Mountain 0. Five units over four moves, no move above 2. The slowest route on the clock and the gentlest on any single step.

Via a hand on the thigh. From Tabletop, step one foot forward into a half kneel, put both hands on the front thigh and push up. The bench has no half kneel, so it counts Tabletop to standing as one move of 3 units. In a room it is two stops with a hand on a solid surface the whole time.

Via a chair or block. The same half kneel, with a hand on a chair seat or a tall block beside the front foot. Have props placed before the class reaches the floor, not after. Whether a particular student should use a chair is their call, informed by their own clinician, never your assessment.

Real world

The quiet student who is always last up. In most mixed classes there is someone who takes noticeably longer to get off the floor and apologises for it. Often they are doing exactly the right thing: stopping on all fours, using a hand, rising slowly. A teacher who cues "take the route up that suits you, I will wait" removes the hurry, and the apology.

Part 3 · covers facing changes, splitting moves, linking poses

Front to side, and the poses that link them

5 min live

Turning the hips from the short edge of the mat to the long edge is a cheap move on its own: Warrior I to Warrior II is 1 unit, facing only. It gets expensive when it is stacked with other changes. Triangle to Vinyasa turns back to the front, puts the hands down and drops a level, all at once: 3 units. The fix is to split it, and the tool for splitting is a linking pose.

LiveSplit the expensive move3 min▶

Triangle right to Vinyasa is the move in Rosa's room where people end up facing different walls. On the bench it costs 3 units: the hips turn back to the front, the base changes from feet to hands and feet, and the level drops from standing to hands-and-feet. Three changes, one cue, one breath.

Put Warrior I right in between. Triangle to Warrior I is 1 unit (facing only: the back heel turns, the hips square up). Warrior I to Vinyasa is 2 (hands down and step back). The total is still 3 units, and the class takes one more breath to cover it, but no single move asks for more than two changes. Each half gets its own simple cue, and the room arrives together.

A split never makes a move free. It trades a little time for a smaller step. Use it where the room stalls, not everywhere.

Self-studyLinking moves and hinge poses2 min▶

A hinge pose shares most of its states with the poses on both sides of it. Warrior II is the classic one for side-facing work: Triangle and Extended side angle are 0 units away (same base, level, facing and side), and Warrior I and Tree are 1 unit away (facing only). Put Warrior II between two side-facing poses and you can move through a whole side-facing family without the hips ever turning back to the front.

Warrior I plays the same role on the front-facing side: it is 1 unit from Warrior II and 2 from a Vinyasa. When you look at a draft and see a 3- or 4-unit move, ask which pose shares states with both ends. That pose is your link. If none exists in your library, the move may simply be in the wrong place, and reordering (which session 4 does on the bench) is the better fix.

Real world

Half the room facing the window. A common sight after a quick "and back to Downward dog" from a side-facing pose: some students turn left, some turn right, some put their hands down first and swivel. Each has solved three changes in their own order. A linking pose like Warrior I gives everyone the same order, so they all arrive facing the same way.

Rewrite Downward dog to Low cobra. Three levels in one move, and the classic place where a room collapses onto the floor at different speeds. Put the knees down first.

★ Step 4 · Downward dog -> Low cobraBEFORE Downward dog -> Low cobra 3 units, one move AFTER Downward dog -> Tabletop 2 Tabletop -> Low cobra 2 TOTAL 4 units, biggest move 2 BREATHS knees down on an exhale, 1 breath in Tabletop, lower over 1 exhale, 3 in Low cobra SAFE chest lifts a little; elbows stay bent; forehead can rest STOP IF wrist pain in Tabletop, or low back pain in Low cobra CUE "Knees down to the mat. Shift forward and lower all the way down. Hands by the ribs, and lift the chest just a little."

Weigh the trade, then rewrite one of yours. Every rewrite here costs more units in total and keeps its floor trip. What it buys is a biggest move of 2 and a place to stop. Then pick the most expensive move in your own opening from session 1 and rewrite it the same way.

Homework

Before session 4

20 min
  1. Draw your own options ladder. Pick one move in your class that stalls the room. Draw three or four stick figures, from most supported to least, each with breaths, a safe range and a Stop-if line.
  2. Find a hinge pose. In your own sequence, look for a 3-unit move and name a pose that shares states with both ends. Score the split.
  3. Practise one floor trip slowly. On your own mat, go from Mountain to Easy seat and back by the routes on this page. Notice where you would want a hand or a block, and plan the props.
  4. Write three transition cues. For three moves in your class, write the words you will say, with the slower route first. Bring them to session 4.
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 3 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · On the bench, Downward dog to Crescent lunge and Downward dog to Low lunge both cost 2 units. Why can stepping straight through still be the hardest move of the night?

Units are a constructed planning scale for states changed. Difficulty for a particular body is a different question, which is why the ladder offers the most supported route first.

2 · Rosa splits Triangle right to Vinyasa by going through Warrior I right. What happens on the bench?

Triangle to Warrior I changes facing only (1 unit); Warrior I to Vinyasa changes base and level (2). A split trades a little time for smaller steps. Warrior I faces the front, which is what makes it the link.

3 · Which sentence about floor trips and injuries can you say honestly?

Swain and McGwin 2016 counted emergency visits by age; nobody has measured whether injuries cluster in transitions. Plan for the people most at risk without inventing a statistic.
