Learn Yoga Flow Design with Phoebe · Session 1 of 6

A flow is a path, not a list

Most first sequences are written as a list of favourite poses. The room does not experience a list. It experiences the moves between the poses: stepping a foot forward, turning the hips, lowering to the floor and getting back up. Tonight we name the four things that change in every move, count what each change costs the room, and look at why a draft that goes down to the floor ten times feels choppy even when every pose in it is good.

Part 0

Where we start

The running case is constructed. Rosa is an invented teacher who has just finished her training, and the Harbour Room is an invented community studio that has given her a 60-minute flow class on Thursday evenings. She already knows how to shape a class: arrive, warm up, build towards a peak, cool down, rest. What she has never done is make one of those shapes move smoothly from first breath to last. Every draft of her Thursday class across the six sessions is something you can write for a real room.

Not medical advice. These pages are about designing the order and pace of a class. They do not teach anyone to diagnose, treat or clear a student for exercise. A student with chest pain, dizziness, a recent injury, or pain that gets worse with movement should stop and talk to a clinician. Session 6 lists the warning signs and the referral route.

Part 1 · covers the four states of a pose

Every pose is a state, every move a change

5 min live

The shape of a class (arrival, warm-up, the main work, a peak, cool-down, rest) is taught in Yoga Theory session 7 ↗. Here we zoom in one level. Whatever a pose looks like, the body arrives in it carrying four facts: what it stands on, how high it is, which way the hips face, and which side is leading.

LiveThe transition is the unit of a flow3 min▶

Write down "Warrior I, Warrior II, Triangle" and it looks like three poses. In the room it is two moves: open the hips from the short edge of the mat to the long edge, then straighten the front leg and reach. The poses are where the room rests; the moves are where it works, balances, and either follows you or gets lost. A flow teacher designs the moves first and lets the poses fall where the moves lead.

The four states give you words for each move. Warrior I to Warrior II changes facing only. Downward dog to a low lunge changes base (hands and feet to a knee down) and level (2 to 1). Mountain to Low cobra changes base and drops three levels at once. The more that changes in one move, the more time, instruction and attention it costs.

Self-studyWhy "vinyasa" is about placement, not speed2 min▶

Vinyasa krama (Sanskrit, "placing in a special way" or "a wise progression") is often heard as "fast yoga". The idea in the tradition is placement: each step prepares the next. Yoga Theory session 7 covers the lineage and the arc; we use only the practical consequence. A well-placed sequence needs fewer and smaller changes between neighbours, so the room spends its effort on the poses rather than on finding its way to them.

None of this needs a fast pace. A slow, gentle class has transitions too, and a student moving from standing to lying still has to get down safely. The four states apply at any speed.

Real world

The class that "felt lovely but bumpy". A common piece of feedback for new teachers. Students cannot name what was wrong, because each pose was fine. Read the sequence as states and it usually shows up at once: the room went from standing to the floor and back many times, or turned from the front of the mat to the side and back between every pose. The fix is rarely a different pose. It is a different order.

Part 2 · covers transition units and floor trips

Count the cost of each move

5 min live

A rough count is enough to compare two drafts. The Flow Bench in session 4 uses this one, and you can do it in your head: one unit for a change of base, one for each level crossed, one for turning between front and side, one for swapping the leading side. On the bench each unit costs one breath of class time. These are constructed planning numbers, not measurements of anyone's body.

LiveFloor trips are the expensive moves3 min▶

A floor trip is any move that crosses between hands-and-feet or standing (levels 2 and 3) and kneeling, sitting or lying (levels 1 and 0). It is where a room spreads out in time: some students are down in two breaths, others take five and use their hands on a thigh or a block. Rosa's first draft has 10 of them in an hour. Bridge straight back up to Mountain and Revolved crescent lunge straight down to Locust each cost 4 units on their own.

Rung 4 on the bench keeps almost every pose and changes the order: all the standing work together, then one move down to the floor, and the floor work grouped from kneeling to lying. Floor trips fall from 10 to 2, total transition units from 72 to 60, and the largest single move from 4 units to 3. Nothing was cut to get there.

Self-studyWhy a rough count is still worth doing2 min▶

The units are a teaching scale. A real move from Downward dog to a lunge takes some people one breath and some people four, and nobody has measured transition time across real classes. The count is still useful for the same reason a recipe's prep time is useful: it lets you compare two drafts on paper before you test one on a room. If one draft scores 72 and the other 60, the second will almost always feel smoother.

What the count cannot tell you is which moves are hard for your particular students. A move that changes only one state can still be the hardest of the night, such as stepping a foot forward from Downward dog for someone with tight hips. Session 3 draws the options for the moves that most often stall a room.

A sentence for your planning notebook. "Group by level, then by facing, then by side." It is not a law, and a skilled teacher breaks it on purpose. As a first draft habit it removes most of the bumps before anyone steps on a mat.

Part 3 · covers Cramer 2015, Swain 2016, Wiese 2019

What the safety numbers do and do not say

5 min live

Moves are where you ask a room to change position quickly, so it is natural to ask whether they are where people get hurt. The honest answer is that nobody has measured that. What has been measured is how often yoga hurts people at all, which body regions, and who is most at risk. Plan with those numbers, and do not invent the rest.

LiveThree studies, three different questions3 min▶

Is yoga riskier than other exercise? A 2015 review by Holger Cramer's team pooled 94 randomised trials with 8,430 participants that reported adverse events. Yoga showed no difference from usual care or exercise in non-serious or serious events, and the authors concluded it "appears as safe as usual care and exercise". It did have more non-serious events than psychological or education programmes, which involve no movement at all.

Who ends up in hospital? Swain and McGwin counted 29,590 yoga-related emergency visits in the US from 2001 to 2014. Most were trunk sprains and strains, and in 2014 the rate for people aged 65 and over was 57.9 per 100,000 participants against 11.9 for ages 18 to 44.

Who reports injuries over a lifetime of practice? In a 2019 survey of 2,620 self-selected practitioners, 45 percent reported no injury ever; of those injured, 9 percent called it severe (4 percent of everyone). More years of practice and teaching yoga both predicted reporting an injury. A convenience sample can only show patterns, not rates.

Self-studyWhat to do with a gap in the evidence2 min▶

No study we could find measured whether injuries happen more in transitions than in held poses. It is tempting to say "most injuries happen in transitions" because it sounds right. Do not say it. What you can say truthfully is that floor trips ask the most of the people most at risk (older students getting down and up), that trunk sprains and strains are the most common emergency injury, and that both are reasons to make moves smaller and to offer a slower route.

The same discipline applies to screening and referral. Intake questions and caution flags for a whole programme belong to Yoga Course Design session 2 ↗; session 6 here covers what to do in the moment.

Real world

The gentle class that was not gentle to get through. A class labelled "gentle" or "seniors" is often built from easy poses and then strung together in any order. Each pose suits the room; the route between them asks a 70-year-old to go down to the floor and back up eight times. Counting floor trips is one of the kindest things you can do for that room.

Build-along

Map the opening of Rosa's class

★ 22 min · everyone builds

Rosa's first draft opens with arrival, a warm-up on the floor, sun salutations and her first standing line. You will map it state by state, count the units by hand, and then do the same for the opening of a class of your own. Pen and paper is enough. The Flow Bench in session 4 will check your arithmetic.

Write Rosa's opening as a column of poses. Leave a gap between every pair of lines. The gap is where the move lives, and it is where you will write.

★ Step 1 · the list (constructed)Easy seat (arrival) Cat-cow Child's pose Downward dog Mountain Sun salutation A x6, Sun salutation B x5 Warrior I R Warrior II R Extended side angle R Triangle R Vinyasa (plank to dog), Downward dog

Tag every pose with its four states. Base, level, facing, side. Floor poses have no facing. Two-sided poses get R or L.

★ Step 2 · statesEasy seat seat, 1, -, - Cat-cow knees, 1, -, - Child's pose knees, 1, -, - Downward dog hands+feet, 2, front, - Mountain feet, 3, front, - Warrior I R feet, 3, front, R Warrior II R feet, 3, side, R Triangle R feet, 3, side, R Vinyasa hands+feet, 2, front, -

Score each gap. +1 base, +1 per level crossed, +1 front-to-side turn, +1 side swap. Mark any gap that crosses between levels 2-3 and levels 0-1 as a floor trip.

★ Step 3 · unitsEasy seat -> Cat-cow 1 (base) Cat-cow -> Child's pose 0 Child's pose -> Downward dog 2 (base, level) FLOOR TRIP Downward dog -> Mountain 2 (base, level) Mountain -> suns -> Warrior I 0 Warrior I R -> Warrior II R 1 (facing) Warrior II R -> Triangle R 0 Triangle R -> Vinyasa 3 (base, level, facing) TOTAL so far: 9 units, 1 floor trip

Find the most expensive gap and try one fix. In Rosa's opening it is Triangle to Vinyasa at 3 units: the room turns back to the front, puts its hands down and steps back, all at once. Write one alternative and score it. You do not have to adopt it; the point is to see the cost move.

★ Step 4 · one alternativeTriangle R -> Warrior II R 0 Warrior II R -> Warrior I R 1 (facing back to front) Warrior I R -> Vinyasa 2 (base, level) Same 3 units, spread over 2 moves: easier to cue, one more breath of class time.

Now map the opening of your own class. Ten to twelve items, real or planned. Tag, score, total, and circle your floor trips. Keep it; session 2 puts a clock on it and session 4 runs it on the bench.

★ Step 5 · your openingITEM BASE LEVEL FACING SIDE UNITS IN 1 ____________ ____ __ ____ __ - 2 ____________ ____ __ ____ __ __ 3 ____________ ____ __ ____ __ __ ... TOTAL UNITS: ____ FLOOR TRIPS: ____ MOST EXPENSIVE MOVE: ______________ (__ units)

Do not chase zero. A flow with no transitions is a single held pose. Moves are the point of a flow class. The aim is to spend units where they buy something (a new shape, a change of pace, a deliberate challenge) and not on avoidable back-and-forth.

Homework

Before session 2

20 min
  1. Finish mapping your opening. All items tagged, every gap scored, floor trips circled.
  2. Read a published sequence as states. Any class plan from a book, a teacher's blog or a video. Count its floor trips. Where would you regroup it?
  3. Take a class as a student and watch the moves. Note the move where the room spread out the most. Which states changed in it?
  4. Time one breath. Sit quietly for a minute and count your breaths. Write the number down; session 2 starts from it.
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 1 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · Warrior I right to Warrior II right changes which of the four states?

The feet stay on the ground, the level stays at standing and the right leg still leads. Only the facing turns, so the move scores 1 unit.

2 · Rung 4 on the Flow Bench keeps almost all of Rosa's poses but groups them by level. What happens to floor trips?

Order, not pose choice, drives floor trips. Transition units also fall from 72 to 60 and the largest single move from 4 units to 3 (constructed bench values).

3 · Which statement about yoga injuries is supported by the studies on this page?

Swain and McGwin 2016. Cramer 2015 found yoga about as safe as usual care and exercise. Nobody has measured whether injuries happen more in transitions, so never claim it.
