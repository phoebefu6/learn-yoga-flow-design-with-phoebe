Adapting a flow live

A sequence that adds up to sixty minutes on paper still has to meet the people on the mats. Some evenings the room breathes slower than you planned, the hour runs away, and one student is clearly not managing a move. Tonight we decide what to do about each of those before class starts: a cut card for the clock, a slower route for the room, and a short, calm rule for the moment a student should stop.

Part 0

Where we start

Rosa is constructed: an invented, newly qualified teacher with a 60-minute Thursday flow class at the Harbour Room, an invented community studio. Her fifth draft works on paper. At 5 seconds a breath it lands on 60:00 exactly, the floor work is grouped, every strong backbend has a counter-pose, and the cool-down is long. Then it is Thursday at 7 pm. The room is breathing slower than her plan, the clock on the wall is already ahead of her notes by the second standing line, and a student near the back keeps coming out of the plank-to-dog move a breath early with a hand on one shoulder. None of this means the plan was wrong. It means the plan needs a second page.

Not medical advice. This page is about adapting the order and pace of a class. It does not teach anyone to diagnose, treat or clear a student for exercise. When a student is in pain, the teacher's job is to stop them, offer rest and refer, never to assess. The warning signs below come from the NHS back pain page; if any appear, the route is 999 or A&E, not a modification.

Part 1 · covers running long or short, the nine-minute swing

Running long or short

5 min live

Session 2 showed the swing: Rosa's fifth draft is 51:00 at 4 seconds a breath, 60:00 at 5 and 69:00 at 6. One second a breath moves the class nine minutes. Nobody has measured the breath rate of a moving class, so you cannot know in advance which room you will get. What you can know in advance is what you will cut, in what order, and what you will never cut. Write it down before class, because nobody makes good cuts with twenty people in Downward dog waiting for the next cue.

LiveWrite the cut card before class3 min▶

A cut card is an index card in your pocket or under your water bottle with three short lists. Cut first: things the class has already had once, such as a second pass of a standing line, extra rounds of a salutation, a repeated hold. Never cut: the cool-down and the final rest, because they are what the room leaves with, and because a student who has been pushed for fifty minutes needs the time to come down. Add if short: things you removed to make the hour fit, in the order you would like them back.

On the bench, Rosa's fifth draft at 6 seconds a breath runs 69:00. Taking out the second standing pass (twelve items repeated at 3 breaths each) saves 5:00. Two fewer rounds of Sun salutation A save 1:48. Dropping the second bridge and knees to chest saves 1:18, and the class now sits at 60:54, inside the bench's one-minute tolerance. One round of Boat instead of two saves another 0:24. Each of those numbers comes from running the engine on a copy of the draft with one change at a time.

Notice what is not on the list. Removing final rest would save 5:00 on its own, exactly as much as the biggest cut. It is still never on the card.

Self-studyWhy these cuts and not others2 min▶

A good cut removes repetition without breaking the shape. After all four cuts the bench still shows 2 floor trips, both sides balanced, no uncountered backbend, Warrior III still the peak, and the cool-down unchanged at 15:12. Transition units fall from 56 to 44, because the second standing pass carried twelve moves of its own. The class is shorter and also a little smoother.

Bad cuts break something the earlier drafts fixed. Cut the left-side Warrior line to save time and the sides are unbalanced again. Cut the Child's pose after Camel and a strong backbend has no counter-pose. Rush the cues to squeeze breaths from 6 seconds to 5 and you are fighting the room's clock instead of reading it. Repetition and the intensity wave are covered in session 5; the cut order follows straight from them: the second pass is the cheapest thing to lose because the room has already met every pose in it.

The short side is harder. At 4 seconds a breath the same draft runs 51:00. Adding back two Sun A rounds, one Sun B round, six breaths on each floor hold and a second Locust with its Child's pose brings it to 55:20. The other few minutes are better found by slowing your own cues than by inventing new poses on the spot.

Real world

The rushed final rest. A familiar pattern in busy studios: the class runs over, the next group is waiting outside, and the teacher shortens final rest to a minute and talks through it. The room leaves with the hurried part. Teachers who keep a cut card tend to lose a second pass at minute thirty instead, when nobody notices, and keep the ending whole.

Part 2 · covers reading the room and slower routes

When the room cannot do the move

5 min live

A held pose shows you who is struggling: you can see it. A flow hides it, because the room is always on its way somewhere. You read a flow by watching the moves, and you have three levers: slow the clock, give a slower route, or split an expensive move into smaller ones, as in session 3. How to plan for a room of mixed levels across weeks, with its research and Universal Design for Learning, is taught in Yoga Course Design session 5 ↗. Here we stay with the minute in front of you.

LiveReading the room in a flow3 min▶

Three things are worth watching, and they are practitioner signs, not measured ones. People waiting: a few students arrive in the pose and look around before you cue the next breath. The room is ahead of you, usually a short-side evening. People holding their breath: jaws set, shoulders up, a pause at the hardest point of the move. The move costs more than the breath you gave it. People coming out early: dropping to the knees, sitting back before the cue, or skipping the move altogether. The move is beyond the room tonight, or beyond one person.

Each sign has its lever. For waiting, keep the pace and use the add list. For held breath, slow the clock: give the move two breaths instead of one, and say so out loud so nobody feels behind. For coming out early, offer the slower route to everyone, not to one student by name: "if you would like, knees down here, or rest in Child's pose and meet us in Downward dog."

The plank-to-dog move is the one rooms most often stall on, and it appears six times in Rosa's fifth draft. The lowering from Plank is often called Chaturanga (four-limbed staff pose). The guide above gives four routes through it. On the bench, the full Vinyasa (plank to dog) is 4 breaths; the Child's pose route costs 2 units going down from Plank and 2 coming back up to Downward dog, so a student who takes it is back with the room on the next breath or two.

Self-studySlow, offer, split2 min▶

Slow the clock. Moving the whole room from 5 to 6 seconds a breath is the gentlest change you can make, and also the most expensive: on Rosa's draft it adds nine minutes. That is what the cut card is for. Slowing and cutting are a pair.

Offer the slower route. Teach it once, early, when the room is fresh, so later you only need the word "options". A route taught for the first time in the middle of the hardest move is just one more instruction.

Split the expensive move. Session 3 drew the splits: a hand on the thigh for stepping forward from Downward dog, the back knee down on the way to a lunge, a stop on all fours on the way to the floor. The bench count often stays the same or rises by a breath; the benefit is that each step changes one thing at a time, which is easier to follow and easier to stop in the middle of.

Real world

The student who stays in Child's pose. A common worry for new teachers is that offering rest looks like giving up on someone. In practice, a room that has been told in the first ten minutes that Child's pose is always available tends to use it briefly and come back. The student who was never told tends to push through, or leave.

Part 3 · covers Cramer 2015, Swain 2016, Wiese 2019, NHS back pain

Stop, adapt, refer

5 min live

Most evenings nothing goes wrong. The numbers below keep that in proportion, and they also say who is most at risk. When something does go wrong, the teacher's job has three steps and no fourth: stop the student, offer rest, and refer if it does not settle or if an urgent sign appears. Screening questions and caution flags for a whole programme belong to Yoga Course Design session 2 ↗. This is the minute in class.

LiveThe in-the-moment rule3 min▶

Stop them. Go to the student, kneel to their level and speak quietly. "Let's pause here." Give the room a simple cue so it can keep moving without you: "three breaths in Downward dog, then step forward to the top of your mat."

Offer rest. Child's pose, lying on the back with knees bent, or sitting: whatever position the student finds easiest. Do not move their body for them.

Refer. If the student settles and wants to continue, offer the slower route or a smaller range and check in after class. If pain stays, they stop practising for the evening and you suggest they speak to a GP before the next class. If any NHS urgent sign appears (both legs numb or weak, loss of feeling around the genitals or anus, bladder or bowel changes, chest pain, or pain after a serious accident), the route is 999 or A&E. You never ask them to show you the movement again, name a cause, or tell them it is nothing. A teacher who assesses is guessing, and a wrong guess is worse than a referral.

Self-studyKeep the numbers in proportion2 min▶

As safe as exercise. Cramer and colleagues pooled 94 randomised trials with 8,430 participants that reported adverse events and found no difference between yoga and usual care or exercise; they concluded yoga "appears as safe as usual care and exercise". Stopping a student is not a sign that yoga is dangerous. It is ordinary care.

Age matters most. Swain and McGwin counted yoga-related US emergency visits; in 2014 the rate was 57.9 per 100,000 participants for people 65 and over against 11.9 for ages 18 to 44. If your Thursday room is older, the slower routes and the stop rule earn their place most.

Most practitioners report no injury, and severe ones are rare. In a 2019 survey of 2,620 self-selected respondents, 45 percent reported never being injured, and severe injuries were 4 percent of all respondents. It is a convenience sample: the people who chose to answer may differ from everyone else, so read it as a pattern, not a rate. None of these studies measured where in a class injuries happen, so do not claim that transitions or holds are the risky part.

Real world

"I'm sure it's just a pulled muscle." A sentence new teachers often say to reassure someone, and one experienced teachers learn to avoid. It sounds kind, but it is a diagnosis. "Let's stop for tonight. If it is still sore tomorrow, a GP or physiotherapist is the right person to look at it" is just as kind and stays inside the job.

Build-along

Write Rosa's cut card, then yours

★ 22 min · everyone builds

You will write Rosa's card for her fifth draft, then one struggle plan for each of her three most expensive moves, then the same for your own class. An index card is the right size: if it does not fit on one card, it will not get read at 7.40 pm. Every number on Rosa's card comes from the Flow Bench and is constructed.

Write the swing at the top of the card. Rosa's fifth draft at the three planning clocks. These are the bench's modelled totals, not a prediction for any real room.

Write the add list for a fast room. Things you took out to make the hour, in the order you want them back.

★ Step 4 · if short (4 s: 51:00)1 Sun A back to 6 rounds +1:12 2 Sun B back to 5 rounds +1:08 3 Pigeon R, L, Seated fold +6 br +1:12 4 Second Locust + Child's pose +0:48 Now 55:20. Slow the cues; finish early with a full rest.

Write one struggle plan for each of the three most expensive moves. In Rosa's fifth draft they are Triangle to Vinyasa (plank to dog) at 3 units (it happens four times), Seated twist left to Supine twist right at 3 units, and the floor trips between Downward dog and Child's pose at 2 units each. Homework

After session 6

20 min
  1. Finish your cut card. Bench totals at 4, 5 and 6 seconds, three cuts with savings, the never-cut list, two adds.
  2. Teach the slower route early. Next time you teach or practise with a friend, introduce knees down and Child's pose in the first ten minutes. Watch whether people use them later.
  3. Rehearse the stop rule out loud. Say the three steps and the five NHS urgent signs from memory, then check them against Part 3.
  4. Check the clock once. In your next class, look at the wall clock at the end of your second standing line and write down how far ahead or behind you were.
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 6 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · Rosa's room is breathing at about 6 seconds a breath, so her fifth draft is heading for 69:00. What does her cut card take out first?

Cut what repeats, never the ending. Removing final rest saves the same 5:00 but is never on the card, and rushing the cues fights the room's clock (bench values, constructed).

2 · Several students are holding their breath and dropping out of the plank-to-dog move early. What is the best response in the moment?

Slow the clock and offer the slower route to the whole room. The cut card covers the minutes that slowing costs.

3 · After a forward fold, a student says both legs have gone numb and weak. What does the teacher do?

Both legs numb or weak is on the NHS Back pain urgent list (reviewed 5 March 2026). The teacher stops, offers rest and refers; never tests or names a cause.
