Part 0

Where we start

Rosa is constructed: an invented, newly qualified teacher with a 60-minute Thursday flow class at the Harbour Room, an invented community studio. On the Flow Bench in session 4 her fifth draft landed on 60:00 at 5 seconds a breath, with two floor trips and every side balanced. It fits. Now she wants it to rise and fall on purpose, so the room climbs to something, meets it, and has time to come all the way down. To do that honestly she needs two things: a sense of how hard yoga actually is, and a way to see where the effort sits in her own hour.

Not medical advice. The intensity numbers below describe groups of healthy volunteers in research settings. They are for planning the shape of a class, not for prescribing exercise to anyone or clearing a student to take part. A student with chest pain, dizziness, unusual breathlessness, or pain that gets worse with movement should stop and talk to a clinician. Session 6 covers what to do in the moment.

Part 1 · covers Larson-Meyer 2016, Hagins 2007, Sherman 2017

How hard is a flow, really?

5 min live

Intensity in exercise research is often given in METs: multiples of the energy a person uses sitting quietly at rest. Under 3 METs is usually called light, 3 to 6 moderate. Three studies, read in their abstracts, give a consistent picture: most yoga is light, a flowing class can reach moderate, and sun salutations are the part that climbs highest.

LiveMostly light, sometimes moderate3 min▶

The review. Larson-Meyer pulled together 17 studies that measured energy use directly from the breath (indirect calorimetry). Yoga practice averaged 3.3 ± 1.6 METs, with a range from 1.83 to 7.4. Take out the single outlier, a study of sun salutations at 7.4 METs, and the average drops to 2.9 ± 0.8. Individual poses averaged 2.2 METs and breathing exercises 1.3. The review's own summary: yoga "is typically classified as a light-intensity physical activity", with a few sequences, sun salutations among them, reaching moderate to vigorous.

A gentle class, measured. Hagins and colleagues put 20 intermediate-to-advanced practitioners in a respiratory chamber for a 56-minute beginner-level video class. It came to 2.5 METs and an average heart rate of 93.2 beats a minute, which was 49.4% of predicted maximum: about the cost of walking at 3.2 km/h. The authors suggested that sun salutation bouts of more than 10 minutes "may contribute some portion" of sufficiently intense activity.

A flow class against walking. Sherman and colleagues measured 38 adults through a 60-minute flowing class and on a treadmill. The class averaged 3.6 ± 0.6 METs, which the authors judged moderate intensity. Brisk self-paced walking cost 4.4 METs, and walking at the same heart rate the class produced cost 4.7.

Self-studyWhy heart rate overstates a flow class3 min▶

In Sherman's study the flow class and the heart-rate-matched walk raised heart rate to the same level, yet the walk used more energy. In a flow class, heart rate climbs for reasons other than pure work: arms held overhead, balance, inversions and breath effort all push it up. So a class that "felt like cardio" by heart rate was, in energy terms, a little less than brisk walking.

This matters for how you describe a class. A heart-rate watch on a student's wrist will make a flow class look harder than it is in energy terms. It also matters when you read other studies: Tsopanidou's numbers in Part 2 are estimated from heart rate, not measured from the breath, so they carry this caveat.

None of these studies tells you how hard your Thursday room will find your class. The volunteers were mostly experienced and healthy, the classes were particular styles, and a new student can find a "light" class hard. The numbers set expectations; your room's breathing, faces and pace tell you the rest.

Real world

The timetable that promises a workout. A familiar pattern: a studio labels a flow class "high intensity" or "burn" and new students arrive expecting a running-level effort. The measured average for a whole flowing hour is closer to brisk walking or a little below it, with the climbs concentrated in the sun salutations. Describing a class truthfully ("steady, with two harder stretches") sets expectations the room can meet.

Part 2 · covers Cheruka 2023, Mody 2011, Tsopanidou 2020, the bench wave

Where the effort sits inside a class

5 min live

An average hides the shape. The same hour can be one long plateau or a climb, a peak and a long descent. Where to put the peak in a class is a design question taught in Yoga Theory session 7 ↗ as the arc and the peak-pose method. Here we look at the measurements that sit underneath that design, and at what the Flow Bench shows for Rosa's hour.

LiveSequences differ, and rounds climb3 min▶

Different parts of one class cost different amounts. Cheruka and colleagues measured 40 experienced adults through a 60-minute flowing class built from named sequences (Baptiste's "Journey into Power"). Energy cost and heart rate differed across the sequences, and the integration and restorative sequences had the lowest heart rate; balancing and back-bending sequences had similar energy cost. The abstract does not give a number for each sequence, so the honest claim is the pattern: a class has a shape, and its quiet ends are measurably quieter.

Repeated rounds push heart rate up. Mody followed six young adults, all trained for two years or more, through four rounds of sun salutation. Heart rate reached 80% of maximum in round 2, 84% in round 3 and 90% in round 4. Six trained people is a tiny sample and says little about a mixed beginner room, but the direction is what a teacher sees: each extra round of the same sequence costs more than the last.

The sun salutation section was the high point. Tsopanidou and colleagues followed 24 moderately trained adults through a 90-minute group class in four sections. The high-intensity sun salutation section had the highest energy rate, and the class averaged 7.1 ± 2.6 kcal a minute. Those figures are estimated from heart rate, which Part 1 showed can overstate a flowing class.

Self-studyReading Rosa's wave on the bench3 min▶

The bench gives every pose an effort level on a 1 to 5 teaching scale. It is constructed: a planning judgement, not a measurement, and Rosa's library tops out at 4. The bench calls the first item at the highest effort the peak, and measures where it starts as a share of moving time (the 10-minute talk budget is left out). The cool-down is the unbroken run of effort-2-or-less items at the very end.

Rung 5 first reaches effort 4 in Warrior III at 26:05, 52% of its 50 minutes of moving time, and its cool-down is 13:30. Rosa's first draft looked different: its first effort-4 item was Camel, at 60% of moving time, and its cool-down was only 8:35, because a Bridge late in the floor work broke the run of quiet items. Grouping the floor work and moving the last Bridge earlier is what gave rung 5 its long descent.

Two things the bench does not know. First, whether the room feels the peak where the scale says: Mody's rounds suggest four rounds of sun salutation can be as demanding as a hard balance, so a high plateau at 8 to 14 minutes may be the real effort high point for some students. Second, what the "right" cool-down length is. No study we read measured that. Treat 13:30 as Rosa's choice, not a target.

Real world

The class that peaks in minute ten. A common pattern: a teacher opens with six or more rounds of sun salutation because "that is how flow classes start", and the room's breathing is at its hardest before the standing work has even begun. On paper the peak pose comes later; in the room the peak was the suns. Counting rounds, and remembering that each round costs more than the last, is often the first fix.

Part 3 · covers repeat-and-add within one class

Teach it once, repeat it, add one

5 min live

A new line of poses is hard to follow the first time: students watch their neighbours, look up for the next cue and lose the breath. A common teaching pattern inside one class is to teach a short line once, slowly, repeat it with less talk, and then add one pose each round. It is practitioner convention. The research below is about practice across sessions, and the jump from it to one class is our inference.

LiveThe pattern, and Rosa's second pass3 min▶

Round 1, teach it. Two poses, slowly, with a demonstration and full cues. Five breaths a pose gives you time to talk the room into the shape and look around.

Round 2, repeat with breath, add one. Fewer words, one cue per move, and one new pose on the end. Many teachers pair each move with an inhale or exhale here; that pairing is a convention taught in Yoga Theory session 7 ↗, not a research finding, and nobody has measured breath rate in a moving class.

Round 3 and on. Add one more. Choose additions that cost 0 or 1 units from the last pose, so the new piece is the shape, not the route to it.

Rosa's bench example. Rung 5 teaches her standing line (Warrior I, Warrior II, Extended side angle, Triangle, Vinyasa (plank to dog), Downward dog) on each side at 4 to 6 breaths a pose: 5:50 of moving time on the bench. Her second pass repeats the same line at 3 breaths a pose: 4:00, with the same 10 transition units. The repeat is shorter because Rosa chose 3 breaths a pose: the room already knows the shapes, so she plans less settling and cueing time. That is a planning choice, not a measurement. Her draft repeats without adding; the add step is yours to try in the build-along.

Self-studyWhat the motor-learning research does and does not say3 min▶

The research on how people learn movement skills is owned by Yoga Course Design session 3 ↗, which teaches it properly. In brief: Shea and Morgan (1979) found that practising skills in a mixed (random) order led to better retention and transfer than practising one skill in a block. Brady's 2004 review put the overall effect at 0.38, and at 0.19 in applied settings, a much smaller advantage outside the lab. Guadagnoli and Lee (2004) proposed a challenge point: learning works best when difficulty suits the learner's current skill.

All of that is about practice spread across sessions and weeks, mostly in laboratory skills. Repeat-and-add inside one class is a practitioner convention. Our inference is only that its logic fits the challenge-point idea: the first round is easy enough to learn, and each added pose raises the challenge a notch. No study we found tested repeat-and-add inside a yoga class, so present it as a craft habit, not as proven.

One more caution from Part 2: repetition raises effort. Mody's six trained volunteers climbed from 80% to 90% of maximum heart rate across four rounds of sun salutation. A line repeated three or four times is a climb, so place your repeats where you want the wave to rise.

Real world

The long new sequence taught once. A familiar scene: a teacher demonstrates an eight-pose line at full speed, then asks the room to flow it on the breath. Half the room is a pose behind, watching a neighbour. The same eight poses taught as two poses, then three, then four, with the rest added over later rounds, usually lands with most of the room moving together by round four.

Build-along

Draw the wave of your own class

★ 22 min · everyone builds

You will turn a class plan into a wave: blocks along the bottom, effort up the side, the peak and the cool-down marked. Rosa's rung 5 is the worked example. Use a class you teach or one you are planning; pen and paper is enough, and the Flow Bench in session 4 will check the numbers if you type the sequence in.

List your blocks with their minutes. Five to eight blocks, each a stretch of the class with one job: arrive, warm up, suns, standing, balance, floor, hips, rest. Use moving time only; leave the talking out.

★ Step 1 · blocks (Rosa, constructed)warm-up 0:00 -> 5:05 sun salutations 5:05 -> 13:45 (4 A, 4 B) standing x2 13:45 -> 23:55 (pass 1, then pass 2) balance, twist 23:55 -> 30:35 floor 30:35 -> 37:20 (Camel, Locust, Boat, Bridge) hips, twists 37:20 -> 45:00 final rest 45:00 -> 50:00 moving time 50:00

Give each block a top effort from 1 to 5. Use the bench scale: 1 rest and arrival, 2 gentle stretches and folds, 3 working standing poses and sun salutation rounds, 4 the hardest balances, backbends and core. The scale is constructed; it is a way to be consistent with yourself, not a measurement of your students. Rosa's library has nothing at 5.

★ Step 2 · top effort per blockwarm-up 2 (Downward dog) sun salutations 3 standing x2 3 balance, twist 4 (Warrior III, Revolved crescent lunge) floor 4 (Camel, Boat) hips, twists 2 final rest 1

Place the peak. Find the first moment your class reaches its top effort and divide its start time by the moving time. A window of 40% to 65% is a planning convention many teachers use, not a research finding: it leaves room to warm up before, and to come down after. If your peak sits outside it, ask whether that is a choice.

★ Step 3 · peak positionRosa rung 5: Warrior III starts 26:05 26:05 of 50:00 moving time -> 52% inside 40-65% Rosa rung 1: Camel at 60% of moving time Yours: peak starts __:__ of __:__ -> ___%

Check the cool-down length. Count backwards from the end: how long has every item been at effort 2 or less? One late effort-3 pose resets the count. There is no measured right length; compare against your own intent and against Rosa's two drafts.

★ Step 4 · cool-downRosa rung 5: from 36:30 to 50:00 -> 13:30 Rosa rung 1: 8:35 (a Bridge at 45:05 broke the run) Yours: from __:__ to __:__ -> __:__ Anything at effort 3+ inside it? ________

Draw it, then compare with rung 5. Shade each block on the empty grid to its effort height. Mark the peak with a dot and bracket the cool-down. Then write one change that would move your wave the way you want it, such as one fewer round of suns, a repeat-and-add line placed before the peak, or a hard pose moved earlier.

★ Step 5 · your wave against Rosa's ROSA RUNG 5 YOURS moving time 50:00 ____ peak pose Warrior III ____________ peak position 52% ___% cool-down 13:30 ____ blocks at 4+ 2 ___ ONE CHANGE: __________________________________

A wave is a sketch, not a heart-rate trace. Your effort numbers are judgements, and the room will not all feel the same thing at the same moment. The value of drawing it is that you see the shape before the room does: a plateau, a peak that arrives in minute ten, or a cool-down that never quite starts.

Homework

Before session 6

20 min
  1. Finish your wave. Blocks, efforts, peak position and cool-down length, all written on the grid.
  2. Run it on the Flow Bench. Type your sequence into the bench in session 4 and compare its peak and cool-down with your hand-drawn ones. Where they differ, which one matches your intent?
  3. Write one repeat-and-add line. Three rounds, two poses growing to four, with breaths per pose and the units between each move.
  4. Watch a class for its wave. Take a class as a student and note the minute your breathing was hardest. Was it the teacher's peak pose, or the sun salutations?
