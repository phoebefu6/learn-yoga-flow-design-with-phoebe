Breath is the clock

A flow class is written in breaths: five in Warrior II, three in Downward dog, eighteen in Pigeon. That makes the breath the clock of the whole hour, and nobody agrees on how long it ticks. Tonight we look at what a breath takes at rest and in a slow breathing lab, admit what has never been measured, use breath as a pacing tool rather than a law, and watch one second a breath move the same class by nine minutes.

Part 0

Where we start

The running case is constructed. Rosa is an invented, newly qualified teacher, and the Harbour Room is an invented community studio where she teaches a 60-minute flow class on Thursday evenings. In session 1 she mapped her class as a path of moves. Every pose on that path has a number next to it, and the number is breaths, not minutes. Tonight we put a clock on those breaths and find out whether her "hour" is really an hour.

Not medical advice. These pages are about designing the order and pace of a class. Breath here is a planning clock, not a treatment: nothing below is a breathing prescription, and nothing promises a change in blood pressure, stress or any symptom. A student with chest pain, dizziness, unusual breathlessness, a recent injury, or pain that gets worse with movement should stop and talk to a clinician. Session 6 lists the warning signs and the referral route.

Part 1 · covers Cleveland Clinic and Mason 2013

How long is a breath?

5 min live

Ask five teachers how long a breath is and you will get five answers, all said with confidence. Two sources give real numbers, each from a different setting. Neither of them is a moving flow class, and that gap is the most useful thing to know about planning by breath.

LiveThree numbers, three settings3 min▶

At rest. The Cleveland Clinic's vital signs page gives the normal adult breathing rate at rest as 12 to 18 breaths a minute. Divide 60 by those and one breath takes 3.3 to 5 seconds. It is a clinical reference for someone sitting still, not a study of yoga.

In a slow breathing lab. Mason and colleagues (2013) asked 17 young, healthy adults who had never done yoga to breathe at 6 breaths a minute, 10 seconds a breath, while seated in a lab. Equal time in and out at that pace gave the largest rise in the baroreflex (the body's automatic blood-pressure reflex) and the largest fall in blood pressure among the patterns they tried. Ujjayi (Sanskrit, "victorious": breathing against a slightly narrowed throat) "requires greater effort" than plain slow breathing, in the authors' words.

In a moving flow class. We searched and found no study that measured breath rate while people moved through a flow class. Say that plainly when someone quotes a number at you. The bench in session 4 uses 4, 5 and 6 seconds a breath because they are reasonable planning values, and it labels them constructed.

Self-studyWhat Mason measured, and what it did not2 min▶

Mason's study is small (17 people), seated, and about breathing as the whole activity. Nobody was stepping a foot forward or holding Warrior II. It tells you that 10 seconds a breath is a pace people can be guided into while sitting, and that ujjayi (Sanskrit, "victorious") adds effort. It does not tell you how fast a room breathes in a standing sequence, and it is not a reason to cue 10-second breaths through a flow.

It also does not make slow breathing a treatment you can hand out in class. Breath used on purpose for stress belongs to Stress Control ↗, and breath practices as their own subject, pranayama (Sanskrit, breath regulation), belong to Yoga Theory session 9 ↗. Here breath is only a clock.

Real world

"Hold for five breaths" is five different lengths. A familiar pattern in any mixed room: the teacher counts five breaths in a calm voice at the front, while a student who has just climbed up from the floor is breathing faster and reaches five long before the cue to move. Neither of them is wrong. The teacher's count is the class clock; each student's breath is their own. Planning by breath works because the teacher keeps the count, not because everyone breathes alike.

Part 2 · covers breath as a pacing tool

Breath as a pacing tool, not a law

5 min live

"One breath, one movement" is the heartbeat of most flow classes: inhale to lengthen or open, exhale to fold or twist. It is a practitioner convention, taught as part of the arc of one class in Yoga Theory session 7 ↗, and no outcome study has tested it. It is a good convention. It is still a convention, which means you may bend it when the room needs you to.

LiveOne breath, one movement, as a convention3 min▶

The convention gives a room a shared rhythm. Lengthening and opening ride the inhale, folding and twisting ride the exhale, and the teacher's voice lands on the turn of the breath. When it works, nobody has to look up to know when to move. That is a real teaching benefit, and you can say so without claiming any study proved it.

The bench uses the same idea as arithmetic: each unit of a move costs one breath of class time. Warrior I to Warrior II only turns the hips, 1 unit, one breath. Downward dog to a low lunge changes base and level, 2 units, two breaths, and it is a floor trip. Triangle to Vinyasa (plank to dog) changes base, level and facing, 3 units, three breaths. The count is a floor, not a ceiling: when half the room is still bringing a foot forward, giving another breath costs 5 seconds and saves the room.

Self-studyBreath-led cueing versus counting2 min▶

Breath-led. "Inhale, reach up. Exhale, fold." The cue follows the breath you hear in the room, so the pace stretches when the room slows. It keeps students inside the convention and is kind to a mixed room, but it makes your timing elastic: the same sequence runs long on a tired night.

Counting. "Stay here for five breaths." The teacher keeps the clock, so the timing is predictable, and students who breathe faster simply take more breaths of their own. The risk is a teacher counting at their own calm pace while the room is working harder.

Most teachers mix the two: breath-led through linked moves, a count in held poses. How to word the cues themselves is Yoga Theory session 8 ↗. Here the point is only that your choice changes your clock.

Real world

The cue that outran the room. A familiar pattern with new teachers: the cue says "exhale, step your right foot forward" and the next cue, "inhale, rise up", arrives while a third of the room is still shuffling the foot between the hands. The teacher is keeping one breath per movement; the room is not. The fix is not a new pose. It is one extra breath, said out loud: "take another breath here if the foot is still on its way."

Part 3 · covers the breath clock on the Flow Bench

The nine-minute swing

5 min live

Rosa's fifth draft, rung 5 on the bench, is cut to exactly 60:00 at 5 seconds a breath. Count the same breaths at 4 seconds and the class ends at 51:00. At 6 seconds it runs to 69:00. Nothing in the sequence changed. These are constructed, modelled numbers from the Flow Bench, and they explain why one teacher's hour is another teacher's 70 minutes.

LiveWhere nine minutes come from3 min▶

The bench builds class time from three parts: holds (breaths in each pose times seconds a breath, with Final rest fixed at 5 minutes by the clock), transitions (each unit costs one breath), and a talk budget. In rung 5 that is 484 held breaths and 56 transition units, 540 breaths in all. Add one second to every breath and you add 540 seconds: nine minutes. Take one away and you lose nine.

At 5 seconds a breath rung 5 is holds 45:20 plus transitions 4:40 plus talk 10:00, exactly 60:00. At 4 seconds it is 37:16 plus 3:44 plus 10:00, 51:00. At 6 seconds, 53:24 plus 5:36 plus 10:00, 69:00. Rosa's first draft swings the same way, and further, because it has more breaths in it: 54:16, 64:05 and 73:54.

Self-studyThe talk budget nobody counts2 min▶

No breath count covers the welcome, the first time you demonstrate a pose, handing out blocks, or the minute you spend helping someone find a strap. The bench sets aside a fixed 10:00 for all of it, a constructed planning figure. Leave it out and a plan that "adds up to 60" runs over before anyone has moved.

Talk does not scale with breath, which is why the swing is nine minutes and not more. It also means talk is the first place a rushed class gets squeezed, and a hurried welcome is where new students miss what they most needed to hear. Plan the talk as deliberately as the poses. What to cut when you do run long is session 6.

Real world

The class that always loses its final rest. A common pattern: a teacher whose plan "fits the hour" keeps running over and quietly trims the rest at the end to finish on time. The sequence is not too long. The teacher counts breaths slower in front of a room than when planning at a desk, and a second a breath across a whole class is minutes. Timing yourself once, out loud, is cheaper than cutting the rest every week.

Build-along

Clock your breath, then clock Rosa's first 20 minutes

★ 22 min · everyone builds

First you find your own clock: breaths in a quiet minute, then breaths in a minute after some gentle movement. Then you put a clock on the first 20 items of Rosa's rung 5 by hand, breaths times seconds plus units times seconds, and check it against the bench. A timer, pen and paper is all you need.

Count your resting breath. Sit comfortably, start a 60-second timer, and count each full breath (in and out is one). Do not try to breathe slowly or deeply; just count. Divide 60 by your number to get seconds a breath. Compare it with 12 to 18 a minute, 3.3 to 5 seconds, and remember it is one minute of one person.

★ Step 1 · seated, 60 sBreaths in 60 s seated: ____ Seconds a breath (60 / n): ____ s Inside 12 to 18 a minute? yes / no (either is fine)

Move, then count again. Stand and do 10 rounds of the arm sweep in the guide, one breath a round: arms down on an exhale, up on an inhale, only as high as is comfortable. Seated sweeps work the same way. Then sit and count for another 60 seconds.

★ Step 2 · after 10 rounds of movementGUIDE: standing (or seated) arm sweep 10 rounds, 1 breath a round, your own pace Safe range: arms to any comfortable height; shoulder height counts STOP IF: dizziness, chest pain, unusual breathlessness, or pain in a shoulder. Sit down, rest, tell someone nearby. Breaths in 60 s after moving: ____ Seconds a breath: ____ s Change from step 1: ____

Pick your planning clock. Your two numbers are not the room's numbers, and no study tells you a moving class's rate. Choose 4, 5 or 6 seconds as your planning value, write down why, and label it a planning value. Rosa plans at 5.

Clock Rosa's first 20 items by hand. Add the held breaths, add the transition units, multiply both by 5 seconds. The items and units are from the bench's rung 5 (constructed). Sun salutation rounds count as one item each with their breaths times rounds.

Homework

Before session 3

20 min
  1. Clock your whole class. Total held breaths and transition units for every item of your own draft, times your planning clock, plus 10 minutes of talk. Does it fit your slot at 4, 5 and 6 seconds?
  2. Time yourself counting. Read five breaths aloud the way you would in class, with a stopwatch running. Divide by five. That is your real teaching clock; compare it with your planning clock.
  3. Watch a class for the breath. Take or watch a flow class and note one place where the cue moved before the room did. How many extra breaths would have closed the gap?
  4. Find your most expensive move. From your session 1 map, pick the move with the most units. Write how many breaths you will give it, and whether you will cue it breath-led or with a count.

1 · The Cleveland Clinic gives 12 to 18 breaths a minute for an adult at rest. How long is one breath?

60 divided by 18 is 3.3 seconds; 60 divided by 12 is 5. Ten seconds is Mason 2013's slow breathing at 6 a minute, seated in a lab.
